A young Kenyan man recently sent this anonymous message to a chatbot late at night:

“Mbogi wanasema mimi si mwanaume nisipolala na huyo mama… tangu lini mtu ashajiita mwanaume kwa sababu ya kulala na wanawake?”

(“My crew tells me I’m not a man if I don’t sleep with her. Since when did sleeping with women make someone a man?”)

That single sentence may explain more about the state of masculinity in Kenya today than many formal reports ever could.

The message came through Sema na Me, an AI-enabled WhatsApp companion developed by Shujaaz Inc. that allows young people to anonymously discuss relationships, sex, mental health and identity in Sheng without fear of judgement. Designed primarily as a safe and youth-friendly support platform, it has also become something else: an unusually candid window into the emotional realities many young Kenyan men rarely express publicly.

Between 30 October 2024 and 12 June 2026, young people exchanged more than 400,000 messages with the Sema na Me WhatsApp chatbot with over 180,000 of them being about relationships, employment, mental health, identity and gender.

Our analysis draws from this anonymised platform data and qualitative conversation trends, providing an unusually large evidence base into the concerns young Kenyans voluntarily raise when they believe they are speaking in confidence. Further among users who self-reported demographic information while joining the platform, young men accounted for most of the gender-identified users, although an increasing share of users in 2026 chose not to disclose gender.

Across Kenya today, young men are navigating a rapidly changing social and economic reality with very few spaces to process what it means. Expectations around manhood remain stubbornly fixed: provide financially, suppress vulnerability, prove masculinity constantly and maintain control. Yet the economic and social conditions required to fulfil those expectations are shifting rapidly.

Many young men are struggling quietly in the gap between those two realities.

This is not simply anecdotal. Across the Sema na Me platform, conversations about employment, financial pressure, relationships and emotional wellbeing consistently rank among the most common discussion themes raised by young users. Many of these conversations reveal the same underlying tension: expectations of masculinity remain high while opportunities to fulfil them are becoming increasingly uncertain.

This tension is unfolding at a moment of wider uncertainty for young Kenyans. High unemployment, rising living costs and the social aftershocks of recent youth-led protests have intensified questions around identity, dignity and belonging. Much of that negotiation is now happening online, where algorithm-driven content increasingly shapes how young men understand power, relationships and masculinity.

This reflects a broader trend. According to the International Labour Organization, young people continue to face disproportionate challenges entering and remaining in decent work

At the same time, Kenya recorded one of its deadliest years for women in 2024, with reports of femicide and gender-based violence increasing sharply according to rights groups and media investigations. Young Kenyan men also continue to die by suicide at significantly higher rates than young women, often in silence and with little access to support. The World Health Organization has in its reports consistently highlighted that men account for a disproportionate share of suicide deaths globally.

These crises are often discussed separately. Increasingly, they should not be.

Analysis of more than 2.3 million anonymous conversations suggests these concerns are neither isolated nor rare. Anonymous youth engagement platforms such as Sema na Me provide one of the clearest windows into how young people discuss masculinity when they believe they will not be judged. The anonymity matters. Users know they are not speaking to a parent, teacher, politician or religious authority. They are speaking into a neutral and non-judgmental space that responds in their own language and on platforms they already use daily.

As a result, conversations that might otherwise remain hidden begin to surface.
