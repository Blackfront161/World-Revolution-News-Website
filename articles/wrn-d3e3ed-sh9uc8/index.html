The answer to a growing mental health crisis in a world in which screens are omnipresent and social media is fueling a sense of despair among the nation’s young people may be simple: get outside and play.

That’s according to a new report by Kaboom!, a nonprofit that builds playgrounds around the nation. The report was created in collaboration with IndigiFit Kids , a three-year campaign by the Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community aimed at improving the physical and mental health of Native youth in Minnesota.

The report outlines data that shows mental health among the nation’s young people is plummeting, and Native youth are the most at risk. Increased screen time, reduced physical activity, slashed recess times, and more than 28 million kids lacking access to a green space or playground point to a shrinking childhood. From 2017 to 2021, depression diagnoses among children rose by 60%, with anxiety rising by 31%. While the issue touches every community, Native communities bear the brunt: suicide rates among Native youth ages 15 to 19 are double that of white youth. But among the bleak numbers, there is hope: kids who spend 60 minutes a day in nature have a 50% lower risk of mental health issues.

Disconnection

Dr. Mark Baez (Coahuiltecan Tribe) is the president of the Society for Indian Psychologists, whose clinic work focuses on adolescents and children. Baez told Native News Online that the issues driving the plummeting youth mental health are compounded in Native communities by the lasting effects of colonialism: poverty, generational trauma, geographic isolation, and loss of culture through federal campaigns to erase Native Americans.

“Many youth carry both online and historical trauma stressors simultaneously,” Baez said.

Those stressors, he said, result in a sense of isolation, antithetical to the core of many Native cultures and communities: connection.

“As clinicians, some of the signs that we see are loneliness, depression, anxiety, difficulty maintaining face-to-face relationships,” Baez said. “For Native youth, we see a lot of our kids on social media being digitally connected while simultaneously feeling disconnected from their family, community, culture, and peers.”

While putting down the phones and getting outside to play might be part of the solution, Native communities are seriously lacking in accessible, safe play spaces compared to other communities. An assessment of 237 playgrounds on or near tribal lands and urban areas with high Native populations found nearly 70% posed safety hazards, with varying degrees of repair and replacement needed.

Ashley Cornforth, Secretary-Treasurer for the Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux, told Native News Online building play spaces for youth can be part of a tribe’s stewardship of their land and seven-generations planning.

“We have a connection to our ancestral land,” she said. “We’re always going to be here. Play spaces are so integral to getting out and playing, and that physical activity has such a connection to mental health. How do we show that we want to maintain it? We want to make it stay good for our next youth.”

Screen Time

While the effects have been clear for many years, a 2021 Wall Street Journal investigation confirmed what many parents suspected: social media harms children’s mental health, and the tech giants behind the most popular apps are well aware. In recent years, Tribal Nations have filed suit against the social media companies , including Meta, Snap, ByteDance, and Alphabet, for their role in the youth mental health and suicide crisis plaguing Native communities. The suits point to addictive algorithms, gambling-like features, and exploitation of reciprocity.

On top of being designed to draw youth in over and over again, social media can provide ample opportunity for cruel bullying, far from the eyes of adults.

“We think, ‘just walk away, close the screen,’ right?” Baez said. “It’s easier said than done. The exposure to trauma and harmful content is very real: bullying, racism, lateral violence, misinformation. It has real harm.”

While the prevalence of screens in modern life may make screen time feel like an insurmountable obstacle for parents, both Baez and Cornforth said that healing Native youth and bringing them back to their culture starts with adults.
